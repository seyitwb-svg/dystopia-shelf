7 books like We, where the state owns your calendar

Reading list · By Max, the reader behind Dystopia Shelf · Last updated: October 7, 2026

← Dystopia Shelf

Zamyatin invented the scheduled utopia in 1921 — a state that owns your hours before it owns your thoughts. Six descendants that kept the idea running, plus one indie where the calendar itself gets sick.

1. We · Yevgeny Zamyatin (1924)

The one that started the lineage: D-503, a mathematician in the OneState, lives by the Table of Hours and believes there is no problem a formula cannot settle. Then he meets I-330 and develops a soul, which his doctors call a disease and offer to cut out. Orwell read it and wrote 1984 inside three years; the glass buildings and the Bureau of Guardians are the blueprint. Written 1920-21, published in English 1924, banned in Russia until 1988.

2. 1984 · George Orwell (1949)

The inheritor that outgrew the original. Winston Smith keeps a diary, and in Oceania the diary is already the crime. The telescreen schedules exercise, hate, and victory announcements; even love gets rationed into the Junior Anti-Sex League. Orwell took Zamyatin's glass state and made it filthy in a way nobody forgets. Published 1949.

3. This Perfect Day · Ira Levin (1970)

The entire planet runs on one computer named UniComp: it decides your job, your spouse, your monthly treatment, and where and what you eat. Li RM35M4419, called Chip, starts skipping doses and learns what the machine is really for. Levin's longest book and the most underrated: the calendar-as-cage premise, decades before 'smart' governance. Published 1970.

4. The Dispossessed · Ursula K. Le Guin (1974)

Not a nightmare but an argument: anarchist Anarres, where nothing is owned and the calendar belongs to the syndicates, against capitalist Urras, where everything is owned including time. Shevek travels between them and finds the walls are mostly inside. Le Guin's subtitle says 'an ambiguous utopia,' which is the honest version of the genre. Published 1974.

5. The Unit · Ninni Holmqvist (2006)

Sweden keeps 'dispensable' citizens (childless women at fifty, men at sixty) in a comfortable research unit where they donate organs one by one until the final donation. Dorrit moves in and finds gardens, friends, and a schedule that ends. The horror is that the system is genuinely nice about it. Swedish original 2006, English translation 2009.

6. The Memory Police · Yoko Ogawa (1994)

On an unnamed island, things keep disappearing (birds, roses, photographs) and a police force removes whatever your calendar of loss hasn't erased yet. A novelist hides her editor, who still remembers, in a room under the floor. Japanese original 1994, English translation 2019, shortlisted for the International Booker Prize.

7. My Brother on the Moon · Maximilian Coolheit Vorfahrenheit (2025)

Disclosure: I wrote My Brother on the Moon. Its Table of Hours is a sickness: each citizen owes it one hour a day, billed to a different slot. What the sick do inside it is prosecuted as murder, though the bounty brokers put a number on every citizen all the same. Auzel Makadam keeps the cemetery's accounts, and a page he doesn't remember writing now prices him. Dexs is the one name no broker can read, and he comes to the graves like he owns a plot. ~68 pages, free on Kindle Unlimited: amazon.com/dp/B0G1KNPF4X.

FAQ

What should I read after We?

1984 is the direct descendant. This Perfect Day takes the scheduled-life premise furthest — one computer assigns job, spouse, and meals. For a stateless take on the same argument, The Dispossessed.

Is We the first dystopia?

Not strictly the first — Jack London's The Iron Heel (1908) is older — but We (written 1920-21, published 1924) is the template the modern dystopia runs on: the glass state, the Table of Hours, the numbered citizen.

Which of these came first?

We itself, published in English in 1924 and banned in Russia until 1988. Next on this list is 1984 (1949), then This Perfect Day (1970), The Dispossessed (1974), The Memory Police (1994), and The Unit (2006).
