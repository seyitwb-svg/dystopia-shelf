6 dystopias that predicted the internet

Reading list · By Max, the reader behind Dystopia Shelf · Last updated: October 5, 2026

← Dystopia Shelf

Some writers saw the feed coming decades before the feed: the screens, the scroll, the ads, the worm, and the silence after. Six books that called it, ranked roughly in order of how early they called it.

1. The Machine Stops · E.M. Forster (1909)

Forster wrote this when the telephone was young, and he describes it anyway: people alone in underground cells, living entirely through a Machine, holding video calls, ordering beds and food by button, afraid of direct experience. It isn't an exaggeration to call it the internet predicted in 1909. It isn't quite a novel either. It's a thirty-page prophecy.

2. Fahrenheit 451 · Ray Bradbury (1953)

The parlour walls are a room-sized endless scroll: interactive, loud, impossible to look away from, engineered so nobody misses their family. The seashell earbuds are AirPods. The firemen burning books are almost beside the point; Bradbury's real subject is a population that stopped wanting to read.

3. The Space Merchants · Frederik Pohl & C.M. Kornbluth (1953)

A world run by advertising agencies where every drink is spiked for loyalty and the environment is a footnote. Written the same year as Fahrenheit, and the funnier of the two. The ads in it feel algorithmically targeted, fifty years early.

4. Stand on Zanzibar · John Brunner (1969)

Information overload as form: the book itself is cut into feed fragments (ads, news flashes, character chapters, data dumps) so reading it feels like a doomscroll before doomscrolling existed. Brunner's densest prediction, and his most uncomfortable to read now.

5. The Shockwave Rider · John Brunner (1975)

The book that invented the computer worm, complete with a protagonist who rewrites his identity inside a surveillance net and fights back through the same wires. Brunner again: he saw the whole shape of networked society and wrote it while ARPANET had forty nodes.

6. Feed · M.T. Anderson (2002)

The feed is literally in your head: a corporate stream of ads and chatter wired into teenage brains, and the one girl who tries to think against it pays for it. Written before Facebook existed, about the exact numbness it would produce. The only book here whose prediction came true within two years.

7. My Brother on the Moon · Maximilian Coolheit Vorfahrenheit (2026)

Disclosure: I wrote this one, and it earns the slot differently. Nothing in it is predicted. The internet is already gone. Ventenstein is a town of ruins where the feed never came back, and the only thing still transmitting on its own rhythm is a virus that requisitions one hour a day for murder. ~68 pages, free on Kindle Unlimited. Amazon link. It's last because the six above saw the wire coming; this one asks what the silence costs after it.

Where to start

The thirty-page prophecy: The Machine Stops. The one that feels written yesterday: Feed. The one that proves dystopia could be funny: The Space Merchants.
