7 dystopias where the monster is ordinary people

Reading list · By Max, the reader behind Dystopia Shelf · Last updated: October 6, 2026

← Dystopia Shelf

The dystopias on this list skip the dictator entirely. The cruelty runs on the village instead: the people who gather to watch and join in. Each book below puts it in ordinary hands and asks what any of us would do if hurting someone were simply what people did here.

1. The Lottery · Shirley Jackson (1948)

A short story rather than a novel, though it set the template this list follows. A small town gathers once a year to draw lots, and the winner is stoned to death by everyone they know, family included. Jackson never explains why. Nobody in the square asks either. It is simply what happens every June.

2. The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas · Ursula K. Le Guin (1973)

Le Guin gives you a perfect city in short-story form, then shows you the child in the cellar its happiness depends on. Every citizen is told about it eventually. Almost everyone stays anyway. The title tells you what the rest do.

3. The Handmaid's Tale · Margaret Atwood (1985)

Gilead works because ordinary women enforce it. The Aunts do the indoctrinating, and neighbors report on neighbors. The regime's real machinery is a crowd of people who each decided that complying was safer than resisting. Atwood has said she invented nothing that had not already happened somewhere. That is the point of the book.

4. Fahrenheit 451 · Ray Bradbury (1953)

People remember the firemen burning books. They forget who wanted them gone: the public. Montag's wife turns in her own husband rather than lose her parlor walls. In Bradbury's telling, a population happily traded thought for comfort and then voted to keep it that way. The state only had to hold the matches.

5. The Power · Naomi Alderman (2016)

Women develop the ability to deliver electric shocks, and the world's power balance inverts. The new rulers end up behaving like the old. What stays with you are the small moments: ordinary people discovering what they can now get away with.

6. The Unit · Ninni Holmqvist (2006)

In this Swedish novel, childless women over fifty and men over sixty are sent to a comfortable facility where they donate organs until a final donation kills them. Everyone calls it humane, and the facility really is pleasant. The residents still have to die on schedule. That gap between comfort and function is where it gets ugly.

7. My Brother on the Moon · Maximilian Coolheit Vorfahrenheit (2025)

Disclosure: I wrote this one. In Ventenstein, a sickness turns the sick violent for one daily hour, never the same one twice. The court still writes murder; the bounty desk writes the check, and your neighbors profit from your death. Auzel Makadam buries the victims while the ledger prices him like the rest. Then Dexs arrives and stays, the first voice in years that isn't quoting a price. About 68 pages, free on Kindle Unlimited.

Where to start

The Lottery takes fifteen minutes. For a novel-length version of the same idea, The Unit is the coldest of the six.
