7 books like The Memory Police, where disappearance is procedure

Reading list · By Max, the reader behind Dystopia Shelf · Last updated: October 7, 2026

← Dystopia Shelf

Ogawa's island works by subtraction: objects vanish, then the memory of them does, and nobody files a complaint. These seven books all run on removal that arrives as paperwork. Six borrowed, one I wrote myself.

1. Ice · Anna Kavan (1967)

Kavan's last novel is a dream-logic pursuit through a world freezing over: a nameless narrator chases a girl through collapsing countries while ice swallows the map. The Memory Police's colder sibling: things vanish here too, and nobody files a report about it either. Written 1967, published by Peter Owen.

2. The Wall · Marlen Haushofer (1963)

A woman wakes in a hunting lodge to find an invisible wall between her and the rest of humanity, who may or may not still be alive. She keeps a cow, a cat, a dog, and a journal. Ogawa readers will recognize the register: survival here is inventory, not action. Austrian original 1963; English translation 1990 via Cleis Press, reissued by New Directions 2022.

3. Never Let Me Go · Kazuo Ishiguro (2005)

Hailsham looks like a boarding school until the donations start: the students are clones raised for organs, and the novel's horror is how politely everyone, including the donors, accepts the arrangement. The institutional erasure runs through manners instead of police, which is exactly why Ogawa's readers land here.

4. The Unit · Ninni Holmqvist (2006)

Sweden's Second Reserve Bank Unit houses the 'dispensable': childless women at fifty, men at sixty, kept in comfort while they donate organs until the final donation. Dorrit falls in love inside a facility that is about to open her up. Bureaucratic kindness with a scalpel behind it. Swedish 2006, English 2009.

5. Moon of the Crusted Snow · Waubgeshig Rice (2018)

An Anishinaabe community in northern Ontario loses power, phones, and deliveries in October, and realizes by winter that the outside world has gone quiet. Rice keeps the camera on the reserve as southern refugees arrive hungry. The end of the world as reported from the edge of it, erasure of infrastructure instead of objects.

6. Prophet Song · Paul Lynch (2023)

Ireland slides into authoritarianism one decree at a time, and Eilish Stack tries to hold four children together while her husband disappears into detention. Lynch's Booker winner is the one book here that happens in a living room, not a ministry: every removal arrives stamped, appealable by nobody.

7. My Brother on the Moon · Maximilian Coolheit Vorfahrenheit (2025)

Disclosure: I wrote this one. Ventenstein files its dead the way Ogawa's island forgets objects: a sickness invoices one hour of every citizen's day at a slot nobody can predict, and what happens inside it stays murder in the file while the bounty desks pay out. Auzel's ledger is where the city's dead get accounted for; this morning the account showed his own name. Dexs arrives last, an outsider nobody can file, and he stays like he means it. ~68 pages, free on Kindle Unlimited: amazon.com/dp/B0G1KNPF4X.

FAQ

Is The Memory Police dystopian?

Yes, in the quiet register: the island's Memory Police enforce disappearances that everyone pretends not to notice. It is bureaucratic horror rather than spectacle, which is why every book on this list works by process instead of violence.

What should I read after The Memory Police?

The Unit is the closest in mechanism: a state that removes people by category. Never Let Me Go for the same politeness, Ice for the same cold. For a one-sitting version of removal-by-ledger, My Brother on the Moon.

Which of these is the quietest?

The Wall: an invisible barrier, one woman, a cow, and a journal. Nothing happens loudly in it, including the end of the world.
