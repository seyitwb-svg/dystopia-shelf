7 books like Fahrenheit 451, where thinking is the crime

Reading list · By Max, the reader behind Dystopia Shelf · Last updated: October 7, 2026

← Dystopia Shelf

Ray Bradbury's fireman who burns books endures because the society he serves chose not to think. If you want fiction where information is controlled and memory itself is dangerous, these seven carry that weight.

1. We — Yevgeny Zamyatin (1921)

Written in 1921 Soviet Russia and banned there for more than sixty years, this is the novel Orwell openly borrowed from for 1984. The One State runs on glass walls and a timetable that schedules even sex. D-503, the engineer building its first spaceship, starts keeping a journal, and the journal becomes the rebellion. The prose is feverish and strange; the book wrote much of the vocabulary later dystopias still use.

2. 1984 — George Orwell (1949)

The obvious companion piece. Bradbury's censorship is passive, a public that stopped reading on its own; Orwell's is active, and the Ministry of Truth rewrites yesterday every morning. Winston Smith's job is to erase people from the record, photographs included, which leaves him among the last who know what actually happened. Every dystopia on this list argues with it, knowingly or not.

3. Brave New World — Aldous Huxley (1932)

Published in 1932, seventeen years before 1984, Huxley's novel works the same fear from the other side: you do not need to burn books when nobody wants to read them. The World State keeps control through pleasure, with engineered babies and a drug called soma for any bad feeling. Bradbury's television walls and Huxley's soma are the same diagnosis, and the diagnosis aged better than either author probably hoped.

4. The Book Thief — Markus Zusak (2005)

Not a dystopia; Nazi Germany needed no invented regime. But it holds Fahrenheit 451's core in another register. Liesel steals books in a country that burns them, and the narrator is Death. It asks what a book is worth when the state has decided books are contraband.

5. The Memory Police — Yoko Ogawa (1994)

On an unnamed island, things keep disappearing: roses, hats, birds, photographs. The Memory Police enforce each new forgetting. A novelist hides her editor, who still remembers, in a space under the floorboards. It is a censorship dystopia without fire or speeches; the mechanism is compliance and snow. If Fahrenheit 451 is about burning books, this one is about losing the words to miss them.

6. The Handmaid's Tale — Margaret Atwood (1985)

In Gilead, reading itself is banned for women, and books are contraband. Offred's narration is the act of witness the regime is designed to prevent. Atwood has said she included nothing that had not already happened somewhere, and that claim is what keeps the novel frightening.

7. My Brother on the Moon — Maximilian Coolheit Vorfahrenheit (2025)

Disclosure: I wrote this one, the least-known book on the list. In Ventenstein a virus arms the infected with one drifting hour a day in which they can kill. The city manages it through a tolerated bounty trade. The gravedigger buries what the hour leaves behind. If what you wanted from Bradbury was the lone conscience inside a system that has normalized something unforgivable, that is the book I tried to write, at about 68 pages. FAQ

Which is most like Fahrenheit 451?

The Memory Police, for the theme of enforced forgetting; We, for the direct lineage to Orwell.

Is Fahrenheit 451 about government censorship?

Partly. Bradbury insisted the book was about television making people stop reading first; the firemen arrive after the public stopped caring.

Which of these is shortest?

My Brother on the Moon at about 68 pages. We and The Memory Police are each under 300.
